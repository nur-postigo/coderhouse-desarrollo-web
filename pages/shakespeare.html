Shakespeare’s Garden

A Study of Botanical Tragedy & Symbolism

Ophelia’s Sorrows

"There’s rosemary, that’s for remembrance; pray, love, remember: and there is pansies, that’s for thoughts." In the floral language of the Elizabethan era, Ophelia’s bouquet was not a random collection of field weeds, but a biting indictment of the royal court.

The Rosemary she offers is a plea for her father to be remembered, while the Pansies (from the French pensées) represent the thoughts she can no longer bear. Shakespeare utilizes the "Language of Flowers" to allow a character who has lost her voice to speak through the soil.

When she mentions Fennel and Columbines, she is speaking of flattery and infidelity—sharp barbs aimed at King Claudius. The Rue she keeps for herself, often called the "herb of grace," signifies the bitter regret and repentance that permeates the final acts of the play.

"I would give you some violets, but they withered all when my father died..."

The Flower of Cupid’s Error (A Midsummer Night’s Dream)

The entire plot of this comedy hinges on a single flower: "Love-in-idleness" (the wild pansy or viola). Shakespeare invents a myth that the flower was hit by Cupid’s stray arrow, turning its petals from white to purple. Its meaning? Irresistible, impulsive love. It serves as a warning that love seen through the eyes alone is a form of delightful madness.

"Before, milk-white, now purple with love's wound, / And maidens call it love-in-idleness."
