The Mourning Bouquet

A Study of Flowers in Sorrow

In the Victorian era, grief was not merely a private emotion; it was a complex social performance. Through the strict "Etiquette of Bereavement," flowers and fashion became the primary language for expressing the depth of one's loss. From the deep matte blacks of "Full Mourning" to the botanical symbols carved into jet jewelry, every element was carefully curated to honor the departed.

The First Stage: Deep Bereavement

During the initial year of "Full Mourning," color was strictly forbidden. Fashion was dominated by Bombazine and Parramatta silk, fabrics that absorbed light rather than reflecting it. The only acceptable "flowers" were those made of dull black crape or woven from the hair of the deceased, symbolizing a connection that transcends the grave.

The Second Stage: Half-Mourning Blooms

As the strict period of grief transitioned into "Half-Mourning," the wardrobe slowly expanded to include muted shades of lavender, heliotrope, and grey. Botanical patterns returned to the hemline, featuring the Weeping Willow and the Forget-Me-Not, signaling a gradual return to the social world while maintaining a respectful memory of the lost.

Glossary of Grave Flowers

Cypress: Despair and eternal sorrow.
Yew: Resurrection and immortality.
Poppy: Eternal sleep and consolation.
White Rose: Worthy of love even in death.
